01 EXECUTIVE OVERVIEW

Two frameworks, one continuous cycle

ODF builds AI-native software. OMG governs the AI assets, models, agents, tools, prompts and knowledge that result. Neither is complete without the other.

What is ODF

OrchestrAI Delivery Framework

ODF is OrchestrAI's software delivery methodology for building AI-native systems: intent and outcome definition, requirements and context capture, AI-assisted design, AI-generated development, testing & QA, and deployment with continuous improvement. Its stated aim is to compress delivery time while keeping validation continuous rather than a final gate.

What is OMG

OrchestrAI Model Governance

OMG is the governance operating system for what ODF (or any delivery process) produces: AI assets, models, agents, tools, prompts and knowledge assets. It records ownership, risk classification, validation outcomes, approval decisions, evidence, certification and reassessment — independent of which delivery process built the underlying asset.

Why they were created as two frameworks, not one

Delivery speed and governance rigor are different disciplines with different failure modes: ODF fails by shipping too slowly or building the wrong thing; OMG fails by allowing an ungoverned or under-evidenced AI system into production. Separating them lets each be evaluated, and improved, on its own terms — while still requiring a defined handoff between "built" and "authorized."

How the loop actually closes today: a governance decision (GO / Conditional GO / NO GO) defines the authorized scope an asset or agent may operate within. ODF (or any delivery pipeline) then builds and ships changes against that scope. When a change is material — a new tool, a new integration, a permission change — it becomes a governance event only once someone records it in OMG as a reassessment trigger. That recording step is currently a process step, not a system-to-system integration.
Speaker notes

The counterpart will likely probe whether the ODF→OMG arrow in Figure 1 is a real API integration. Be direct: it is not, today. The taxonomy of "material change" exists inside OMG (Model Change, New Tool, New Integration, Permission Change, Access Scope Change, Control Failure, Risk Threshold Breach, Performance Drift, Regulatory Change, Policy Change, and others), but population of that taxonomy currently depends on a person or process choosing to log it.

This is the single most important framing for the rest of the deck: OMG's lifecycle is conceptually closed-loop, but mechanically it is closed by human governance process today, not by live delivery or runtime telemetry. That gap is precisely the shape of the conversation this meeting exists to have.

02 CURRENT OMG CAPABILITIES

Seventeen capability areas, in five layers

Every capability below exists as working software in OMG today. Grouping is by governance layer, not by build order.

Foundation

AI Asset, Model, Knowledge, Prompt, Tool and Agent Governance — each governed entity type is registered with a type, owner and risk tier; Lifecycle Management tracks its stage from registration to retirement.

Operations

Ownership & Accountability (a single five-role matrix reused across every entity type, including agents), Risk Governance, Control Governance and Assessment Governance — how oversight is actually exercised.

Assurance

Evidence Governance (polymorphic across all entity types), Certification Governance and Governance Readiness — the proof layer that supports every governance claim.

Intelligence

Regulatory Intelligence — a Source → Requirement → Obligation → Control → Evidence chain that lets a new regulation onboard as configuration rather than a rebuild.

Oversight

Audit & Oversight (an append-only record of governance actions) and Governance Continuity (scheduled reauthorization and reassessment tracking).

Speaker notes

If asked "is this a diagram of your database schema or your actual product," answer: both — each node corresponds to a real, navigable module in the running application, not an aspirational architecture diagram.

Emphasize the reuse of a single Ownership & Accountability model across Agents, Tools, Models, Prompts and Knowledge Assets. This is a deliberate design choice (documented internally as "reuse before extend, extend before invent") rather than an oversight — it means an agent is held to the same accountability bar as any other governed AI system, not a bespoke one.

03 OMG GOVERNANCE OPERATING MODEL

How the pieces operate as a cycle

Nine mechanisms, one lifecycle. The sequence below reflects how they actually operate in the platform, which reorders two items from a naive alphabetical or listed order: risk classification informs how rigorous an approval must be, so it is shown ahead of approval.

Ownership → Accountability

Five named roles (Business, Technical, Risk, Compliance Owner, Approver) assigned per entity. Completeness is measured and reported — an incomplete assignment is visible, not hidden or silently defaulted.

Risk Classification → Approvals

A four-tier risk classification (Low/Medium/High/Critical) sets the expected rigor. Approval is a named human decision — GO, Conditional GO, or NO GO — with a recorded justification.

Evidence → Auditability

Evidence accumulates against any governed entity with its own ownership and expiry. Every governance action taken inside OMG is written to an append-only audit log.

Monitoring

Continuous health scoring across the live portfolio — Healthy, Watchlist, or Attention Required — surfaced on the Executive Dashboard.

Reassessment

A logged trigger (model change, new tool, permission change, control failure, and others) calls a prior authorization into question and routes it back to governance review.

Governance Continuity

Reauthorization status — Active, Due Soon, Overdue, or Expired — computed against a scheduled next-review date, re-establishing whether Ownership and the original approval still hold.

Where this cycle is schedule-driven, not event-driven: Monitoring, Reassessment and Continuity are the three mechanisms most often assumed to be "real-time." In the current implementation, Monitoring is a computed health score over stored data, Reassessment requires a trigger to be logged, and Continuity is computed from a calendar date. None of the three currently subscribes to a live stream of delivery or runtime events.
Speaker notes

This slide is where a sharp technical evaluator will start testing for overstatement. Lead with the callout box rather than waiting for the question — naming the schedule-driven nature of continuity and monitoring before being asked is what establishes credibility for the rest of the deck.

If asked why reassessment is not automatically triggered by tool or permission changes today: this was a deliberate scope boundary, not an oversight — OMG's mandate has been to make governance activity itself complete and evidenced, not to instrument every upstream delivery or runtime system that could produce a change signal.

05 BOUNDED ENTERPRISE SCENARIO

One agent, three points in time

A concrete trace of what OMG observes, records and misses — using the shape of a real governed agent already in the platform's reference data.

T0 — Approved baseline

Agent is registered, ownership assigned across all five roles, risk classified as Critical, independent validation completed and scored, and a GO decision recorded by a named approver.

T1 — Delivery change

A new external tool is added and a new delegation path introduced. Technical testing succeeds. From OMG's point of view, permissions remain valid — its last computed reauthorization status still reads Active or Due Soon, unaffected by the change.

T2 — Consequential action

The agent attempts a consequential action through the newly added tool. No governance system sits in this execution path today.

QuestionAnswer for this scenario
What OMG observesAt T0: the full registration, ownership, risk and approval record. At T1–T2: nothing directly — OMG has no live signal from ODF or from the agent runtime.
What OMG recordsThe T0 approval and its evidence permanently. The T1 tool addition and the T2 action are recorded only if and when a human logs an incident or reassessment trigger after the fact.
What OMG reassessesNothing automatically at T1 or T2. Reassessment begins only once a trigger exists — typically after T2, once an anomaly is reported.
What OMG establishesA new governance record for the incident, an updated operational status, and a corrective action tracked to verified closure.
What governance decisions OMG can makeA human-recorded decision to suspend, reassess, or reauthorize the agent — never an automatic decision, and never before the fact.
What evidence OMG retainsThe original approval evidence, the incident record, the reassessment trigger, and the corrective action's verification notes — a complete after-the-fact record.
Where human intervention occursTwice: once to notice and report the anomaly after T2, and once to make the reassessment decision inside OMG. No automated step exists between T1 and the human report.
Speaker notes

This scenario is deliberately not a worst-case hypothetical — it mirrors a real incident pattern already represented in OMG's own reference data (a multi-agent system whose operational status was suspended and placed under a critical, still-open reassessment trigger after an anomaly, with a corrective action still in progress). The point is not that something went wrong; it is that OMG's design assumes a human will notice and report.

Use this scenario to transition directly into Section 6: the T1–T2 gap in Figure 4 is exactly the space an authority assurance layer would occupy.

06 OMG VS. AUTHORITY ASSURANCE

Two layers, two different moments in time

OMG operates before and around an action. An authority assurance layer would operate at the moment of the action itself.

Governance Layer (OMG, today)Authority Assurance Layer (potential, adjacent)
PurposeEstablish and record that governance activity occurred: ownership, risk classification, validation, approval, evidence, certification.Validate, at or near the moment of action, whether an agent currently holds valid, applicable authority to perform a specific action.
InputsRegistrations, ownership assignments, validation results, human decisions, logged reassessment triggers, review schedules.Live agent/action context, current permission or delegation state, policy rules, real-time system signals.
OutputsReadiness status, decision records, evidence records, certification records, an append-only audit trail.An allow / deny / step-up verdict, a current authority state, an action-admissibility record.
DecisionsHuman governance decisions (GO / Conditional GO / NO GO), typically recorded at milestones, not per action.Automated or semi-automated, per-action admissibility decisions, made continuously.
EvidenceDurable governance evidence tied to milestones (approval, certification, reassessment).Transaction- or action-level evidence tied to individual attempted actions.
EnforcementNone. Findings and recommendations route to a human; nothing is blocked automatically.Enforcement-capable by definition — able to permit or refuse an action in real time.
Ownership (org function)Governance, risk and compliance function.Runtime security / platform engineering function.
Where OMG currently operates: the design-time and periodic-review layer — from registration, through approval, through evidence and certification, to scheduled reassessment. The potential adjacent layer sits at the point of action: the moment between an agent deciding to act and that action executing, which OMG's current architecture does not occupy and was not built to occupy.
Speaker notes

Resist any framing that casts this as "OMG is incomplete." The honest position is narrower and more defensible: OMG and a real-time authority assurance layer answer different questions on different time horizons, using different evidence, for different organizational owners. That is the basis for adjacency, not redundancy.

09 CONCLUSION

Where governance ends, and assurance may begin

What OMG does well today
  • Complete, named ownership and accountability across every governed entity type, including agents.
  • Durable, polymorphic evidence with computed lifecycle and expiry.
  • Independent, multi-discipline validation and assessment, recorded and scored.
  • Named, justified human decisions at every governance milestone.
  • Certification and readiness scoring that is honest about gaps rather than gamed toward a number.
  • An append-only audit trail of governance activity, and cross-domain reporting over it.
  • Scheduled reassessment and reauthorization tracking, with a typed taxonomy for material change.
What OMG intentionally does not yet do
  • Automatically detect material change from live delivery or runtime signals.
  • Validate authority at the moment an action is attempted.
  • Enforce, gate, or block any agent or tool action.
  • Automatically intercept consequential actions before they execute.
Where governance ends: at the boundary between "a decision was made and recorded" and "an action is being attempted right now." OMG is authoritative on the first; it does not participate in the second.

Where authority assurance may begin: exactly at that boundary — validating, at the moment of action, whether the authority OMG recorded is still valid and applies to this specific action.

How the two could operate together: OMG as the system of record and decision authority — who approved what, under what conditions, with what evidence — and an authority assurance layer as the real-time enforcement point that consumes that governance state as input, and feeds observed material-change and action signals back into OMG as reassessment triggers. That would close the loop Section 5's scenario showed is open today, without either layer duplicating the other.
Speaker notes

Close by returning to the meeting's actual purpose: this was never a pitch for OMG to be an authority assurance product, nor an invitation for an authority assurance platform to become a governance-of-record system. The honest reading of the evidence in this deck is adjacency with a specific, nameable seam — Section 6's boundary and Section 7's seven areas — not overlap and not full independence.
